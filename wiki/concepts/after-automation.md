# After Automation

## Summary

"After Automation" is Every CEO Dan Shipper's argument, invoked in the Draft Review Kit article, that AI does not reduce the human workload—it changes what the human does. Automating the easy part raises the bar on the hard part.

## Source-Backed Claims

- The article frames itself as "a microcosm of Every CEO Dan Shipper's argument in 'After Automation'": AI "changes what the human does instead of reducing the workload."
- Concretely, the author must "be clear about defining my standards so a model can understand them," which "creates more work—but it helps me understand them better myself."
- Setting up reviews "takes time, attention, and a certain comfort with a tool like Codex or Claude Code," but once in place frees the author to push a draft "from good to great."
- A related Every piece by the same author is referenced: "AI Was Supposed to Free My Time. It Consumed It." (Mar 9, 2026)—"AI doesn't reduce work—it makes you want to do more of it."

## Interpretation

The thesis reframes the common "machine does more, human does less" bargain. The work shifts upward: from producing prose to specifying standards, building review infrastructure, and adjudicating synthesized feedback (see [Reviewer Orchestration](reviewer-orchestration.md)). This is the conceptual backbone for why [Review Skills](review-skills.md) feel like added work yet are claimed to be worth it—the articulation of standards is itself the payoff. It is the author's lived experience and Shipper's framing, not an established empirical finding.

## Contradictions or Tensions

- The panel on the article's own draft surfaced the unresolved question: is this "an essay about a system that's working, or an essay about a writer who has to keep building infrastructure because her own defaults keep losing?" The "more work" framing cuts both ways.
